# Literature review: economic and weather shocks, conflict, aid, and child wasting/stunting

Prepared for the FEWS NET x DHS concept note (FEWS NET sub-national IPC-compatible classifications and projections, 2011–2024, linked to DHS child anthropometry: wasting as a contemporaneous outcome, stunting as an early-life cohort-exposure outcome).

**Verification key.** [V] means the citation and the headline number were checked against the publisher page, PubMed/PMC, RePEc, or an author PDF during this review (September 2026). [V-cit] means the citation was verified but the effect size is from a secondary summary or abstract and should be re-checked in the full text. [K] means it comes from prior knowledge and was not web-checked in this pass. Please verify [K] items before citing.

## 1. Economic and price shocks and child wasting (Headey and related work)

**Headey, D. D., & Ruel, M. T. (2022). Economic shocks predict increases in child wasting prevalence. *Nature Communications*, 13, 2157.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29755-x (open access; PMC9021262) [V]
Links 1.256 million children in 52 countries (DHS, 1990–2018) to lagged annual changes in national GDP per capita. A 10% annual fall in national income raises moderate/severe wasting prevalence by **14.4–17.8%** (relative, not percentage points). Dietary diversity is the suggested mechanism. Applied to 2020 growth forecasts, it implies about 9.4 million additional wasted preschoolers from COVID-19. *Relevance:* This is the closest template for our design, a pooled DHS wasting regression on a lagged macro shock. It uses only national-level variation, and FEWS NET would add sub-national, sub-annual variation.

**Headey, D., & Ruel, M. (2023). Food inflation and child undernutrition in low and middle income countries. *Nature Communications*, 14, 5761.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-41543-9 (PMC10505228) [V]
Covers 1.27 million children from 130 DHS surveys in 44 countries (2000–2021), linked to national monthly real food price changes from FAO CPI. A **5% rise in real food prices over the previous 3 months raises wasting risk by 9% and severe wasting by 14%**. Effects are larger for ages 0–11 months (11%), for boys (roughly twice the effect for girls), for asset-poor households (15% vs 6%), and for landless rural boys (29%). Food inflation in utero and in the first year raises later stunting at 24–59 months by about 1.6–1.8% per 5%. *Relevance:* It uses the same wasting-now and stunting-cohort split we propose. Its short-window (3-month) wasting result is the benchmark for a FEWS NET "current phase" effect.

**Headey, D., Heidkamp, R., Osendarp, S., Ruel, M., et al. (2020). Impacts of COVID-19 on childhood malnutrition and nutrition-related mortality. *The Lancet*, 396(10250), 519–521.** https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(20)31647-0 [V-cit]
This is a projection exercise (MIRAGRODEP/LiST-style) that estimates **6.7 million additional wasted children in 2020**, 57.6% of them in South Asia. *Relevance:* It shows elasticity-based wasting projections are already used in policy. FEWS NET-based projections could be tested against them.

**Headey, D. (2013). The impact of the global food crisis on self-assessed food security. *World Bank Economic Review*, 27(1), 1–27.** [K; citation partially confirmed via search]
Uses Gallup World Poll data and finds the 2007–08 price spike had surprisingly small effects on self-reported food insecurity, explained by income growth and heterogeneity. *Relevance:* It shows prices and experienced food insecurity can diverge, which is a caution for mapping price or IPC signals onto anthropometry. Note that this paper is about food security, not child anthropometry. The user's "Headey 2013 on food prices and child nutrition" may refer to this paper. The child-nutrition price work is Headey & Ruel (2023).

**Woldemichael, A., Kidane, D., & Shimeles, A. (2022). Food inflation and child health. *World Bank Economic Review*, 36(3), 757–773.** https://doi.org/10.1093/wber/lhac009 [V-cit; pages K]
Uses Ethiopia DHS and finds that exposure to food inflation in utero and in infancy worsens child HAZ/stunting. *Relevance:* A single-country sub-national price-shock design using DHS.

**Mekasha, T. J., Molla, K. G., & Tarp, F. (2022). Commodity price fluctuations and child malnutrition. *World Development*, 158, 105986 (article no. K).** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305750X22001176 [V-cit]
Coffee price shocks in Tanzania and child anthropometry. *Relevance:* Income-side (export crop) price shocks, as opposed to consumer food prices.

**Baird, S., Friedman, J., & Schady, N. (2011). Aggregate income shocks and infant mortality in the developing world. *Review of Economics and Statistics*, 93(3), 847–856.** https://doi.org/10.1162/REST_a_00084 [V]
Uses 1.7 million DHS births in 59 countries. A **1% fall in GDP per capita raises infant mortality by 0.24–0.40 per 1,000 births**, and girls are more sensitive. *Relevance:* The canonical DHS birth-history macro-shock design. The mortality selection it implies matters for interpreting surviving children's anthropometry.

## 2. Weather and rainfall shocks and child nutrition

**Cooper, M. W., Brown, M. E., Hochrainer-Stigler, S., Pflug, G., McCallum, I., Fritz, S., Silva, J., & Zvoleff, A. (2019). Mapping the effects of drought on child stunting. *PNAS*, 116(35), 17219–17224.** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1905228116 (PMC6717288) [V]
Covers 584,662 children in 127 geolocated DHS surveys from 53 countries (1990–2016). The 24-month SPEI predicts HAZ best. HAZ falls monotonically with rainfall deficits, even for minor drought (SPEI < −0.4), and severe wetness also lowers HAZ. The optimum is SPEI of about 0 to +1. Effects are mapped as modulated by governance, trade, and agricultural diversity. The paper reports effects in HAZ units and does not analyse wasting. *Relevance:* It is the main "droughts raise stunting" paper, so our paper should not re-establish this. It also offers a ready-made raw-climate comparator (24-month SPEI) for a horse-race against FEWS NET.

**Anttila-Hughes, J. K., Jina, A. S., & McCord, G. C. (2021). ENSO impacts child undernutrition in the global tropics. *Nature Communications*, 12, 5785.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-26048-7 [V; effect sizes V-cit]
Covers 186 DHS surveys in 51 ENSO-teleconnected countries (1986–2018). Warmer ENSO worsens undernutrition where it dries areas and improves it where it wets them. Per +1 °C in the ENSO index, WAZ falls about 0.03 SD and underweight rises about 0.6 pp. Weight losses show up later as reduced height. The 2015 El Niño implies about 5.9 million additional underweight children. The reported association with WHZ/wasting was not significant (p≈0.21, per secondary citation). *Relevance:* It is a predictable, forecastable climate shock, which is a natural benchmark for "forecast skill". The null on WHZ is a caution about the noise in wasting.

**Randell, H., Gray, C., & Grace, K. (2020). Stunted from the start: Early life weather conditions and child undernutrition in Ethiopia. *Social Science & Medicine*, 261, 113234.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2020.113234 [V-cit; article no. K]
Uses four rounds of Ethiopia DHS linked to high-resolution climate data. More early-life rainfall raises HAZ. Higher prenatal temperatures raise severe stunting, while warmer early-life (postnatal) temperatures are associated with less severe stunting. *Relevance:* Uses the DHS early-life exposure windows we plan for stunting, in a key FEWS NET country.

**Dimitrova, A. (2021). Seasonal droughts and the risk of childhood undernutrition in Ethiopia. *World Development*, 141, 105417.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2021.105417 [V]
Uses Ethiopia DHS and SPEI. Main-season drought raises both stunting and **wasting**. Exposure in the first two years is most harmful, and boys, children of less-educated mothers, and farm households are more vulnerable. *Relevance:* One of the few DHS papers with a wasting result for drought.

**Dimitrova, A., & Muttarak, R. (2020). After the floods: Differential impacts of rainfall anomalies on child stunting in India. *Global Environmental Change*, 64, 102130.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2020.102130 [V-cit; article no. K]
Uses Indian DHS (NFHS) and gridded rainfall. Excess rainfall raises stunting, disadvantaged groups are more vulnerable, and maternal education is protective. *Relevance:* Floods as well as droughts matter, which is non-monotonic like Cooper et al.

**Hirvonen, K., Sohnesen, T. P., & Bundervoet, T. (2020). Impact of Ethiopia's 2015 drought on child undernutrition. *World Development*, 131, 104964.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2020.104964 [V-cit; article no. K]
Uses nationally representative data before and after the 2015 El Niño drought (one of the worst in decades). The drought produced **no widespread increase in stunting or wasting**. Stunting rose only in drought areas with poor road access. The authors credit the large humanitarian and PSNP response and infrastructure. *Relevance:* This is the key "aid dampens measured outcomes" example, in a case where FEWS NET forecast a severe crisis and response followed.

**Abiona, O. (2017). Adverse effects of early life extreme precipitation shocks on short-term health and adulthood welfare outcomes. *Review of Development Economics*, 21(4), 1229–1254.** https://doi.org/10.1111/rode.12310 [V-cit; pages K]
Uses Malawi (World Bank household surveys, not DHS). An in-utero drought, or one in the first or second year of life, lowers WAZ by 15%, 17%, and 43% and HAZ by 14%, 15%, and 27% respectively (relative to mean z-score). Adults exposed in utero have delayed school entry.

**Abiona, O. (2022). Malnutrition pathway for the impact of in utero drought shock on child growth indicators in rural households. *Environment and Development Economics*, 27(1), 20–39.** [V]
Malawi. A pre-gestation growing-season rainfall deficit cuts HAZ about 22%, working through harvest and consumption. Positive rainfall shocks have no symmetric benefit.

**Abiona, O. (2024). Weather shocks, birth and early life health: Evidence of different gender impacts. *Journal of African Economies*, 33(1), 46–66.** [V]
Sierra Leone DHS birth cohorts, 2003–2013. In-utero drought raises low birth weight more for boys, but later stunting effects are smaller for boys than girls, consistent with food allocation favouring boys. *Relevance:* This resolves the "Abiona (Malawi, Sierra Leone)" reference, which is two separate papers.

**Thiede, B. C., & Gray, C. (2020). Climate exposures and child undernutrition: Evidence from Indonesia. *Social Science & Medicine*, 265, 113298.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2020.113298 (PMC7738425) [V]
Uses the IFLS panel (1993–2015, not DHS) with fixed effects. Delayed monsoon onset consistently worsens HAZ and WHZ. *Relevance:* Timing of the season, not just totals, matters, which FEWS NET seasonal monitoring tracks.

**Shively, G. E. (2017). Infrastructure mitigates the sensitivity of child growth to local agriculture and rainfall in Nepal and Uganda. *PNAS*, 114(5), 903–908.** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1524482114 [V]
Uses DHS, n=11,946. A +1 SD in growing-season rainfall raises z-scores by 0.05–0.25, roughly 4–13% for HAZ and 1–7% for WHZ. Roads and health-facility access dampen the sensitivity.

**Blom, S., Ortiz-Bobea, A., & Hoddinott, J. (2022). Heat exposure and child nutrition: Evidence from West Africa. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*, 115, 102698.** [V]
Uses DHS children aged 3–36 months, n>32,000. Mean 3-month heat exposure (470 hours above 30 °C) lowers **WHZ by 0.16 SD** and HAZ by 0.14 SD. The authors attribute about 29% of wasting and 12% of stunting prevalence to average heat exposure. *Relevance:* This is a rare, clean contemporaneous-shock design for wasting. Heat is a channel FEWS NET does not directly classify.

**Baker, R. E., & Anttila-Hughes, J. (2020). Characterizing the contribution of high temperatures to child undernourishment in Sub-Saharan Africa. *Scientific Reports*, 10, 18796.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-74942-9 [V]
Uses DHS for 192,000 children in 30 SSA countries. High temperatures raise wasting, and projections to 2100 imply +37% wasting in West Africa and +25% in Central/East Africa.

**Grace, K., Davenport, F., Funk, C., & Lerner, A. M. (2012). Child malnutrition and climate in Sub-Saharan Africa: An analysis of recent trends in Kenya. *Applied Geography*, 35(1–2), 405–413.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apgeog.2012.06.017 [V]
Kenya DHS. Drying and warming are associated with more stunting. The authors include FEWS NET/CHIRPS climate scientists (Funk).

**Hoddinott, J., & Kinsey, B. (2001). Child growth in the time of drought. *Oxford Bulletin of Economics and Statistics*, 63(4), 409–436.** https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0084.t01-1-00227 [V]
Zimbabwe resettlement panel. Children aged 12–24 months lose **1.5–2 cm of growth** after the 1994/95 drought, with limited catch-up.

**Dercon, S., & Porter, C. (2014). Live Aid revisited: Long-term impacts of the 1984 Ethiopian famine on children. *Journal of the European Economic Association*, 12(4), 927–948.** https://doi.org/10.1111/jeea.12088 [V-cit; pages K]
Ethiopia Rural Household Survey. Children aged 12–36 months at the famine's peak are **at least 5 cm shorter** as young adults, implying about 5% lower lifetime income. The relief operation made little difference to survivors. *Relevance:* Famine exposure maps onto stunting cohorts. Also relevant to the mortality-selection caveat.

**Maccini, S., & Yang, D. (2009). Under the weather: Health, schooling, and economic consequences of early-life rainfall. *American Economic Review*, 99(3), 1006–1026.** https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.99.3.1006 [V]
Indonesia IFLS. For women, 20% higher birth-year rainfall means **0.57 cm taller** as adults, 0.22 more grades of schooling, and household assets 0.12 SD higher (AER abstract, verified). There are no effects for men.

**Kudamatsu, M., Persson, T., & Strömberg, D. (2012). Weather and infant mortality in Africa. CEPR Discussion Paper 9222.** https://cepr.org/publications/dp9222 [V]
Covers about 1 million DHS births in 28 African countries, linked to ERA-40 weather. In-utero drought in arid areas raises infant mortality by **about 2.5 pp**, especially for hungry-season births. A six-month malaria epidemic in low-exposure areas raises it by more than 3.5 pp. *Relevance:* It uses the DHS birth-history design with weather, and the mortality effect is the selection channel for anthropometry.

**Burke, M., Gong, E., & Jones, K. (2015). Income shocks and HIV in Africa. *Economic Journal*, 125(585), 1157–1189.** https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12149 [V]
Links DHS HIV biomarkers for more than 200,000 people in 19 countries to rainfall shocks. Each recent drought raises HIV prevalence about 11% in HIV-endemic rural areas. *Relevance:* A methodological template (DHS plus rainfall shock as an income shock) and evidence that drought shocks travel through non-food channels.

## 3. Conflict and child nutrition

**Akresh, R., Verwimp, P., & Bundervoet, T. (2011). Civil war, crop failure, and child stunting in Rwanda. *Economic Development and Cultural Change*, 59(4), 777–810.** https://doi.org/10.1086/660003 [V]
Children born during the war in conflict regions have **HAZ 1.05 SD lower**. Crop failure lowers HAZ by 0.86 SD for girls only.

**Bundervoet, T., Verwimp, P., & Akresh, R. (2009). Health and civil war in rural Burundi. *Journal of Human Resources*, 44(2), 536–563.** https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.44.2.536 [V]
Each additional month of war exposure lowers HAZ by **0.047 SD**.

**Minoiu, C., & Shemyakina, O. N. (2014). Armed conflict, household victimization, and child health in Côte d'Ivoire. *Journal of Development Economics*, 108, 237–255.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2014.03.003 [V]
Uses DHS before, during, and after the 2002–07 war, with ACLED-type event data. HAZ is **0.2–0.4 SD lower** in conflict regions, and household economic victimization is a key channel.

**Dunn, G. (2018). The impact of the Boko Haram insurgency in Northeast Nigeria on childhood wasting: a double-difference study. *Conflict and Health*, 12, 6.** https://doi.org/10.1186/s13031-018-0136-2 [V]
Uses Nigeria DHS 2008 vs 2013 in a difference-in-differences design. Conflict lowers mean **WHZ by 0.49 SD** and raises wasting by **13 pp** (23% vs a 10% counterfactual). *Relevance:* One of the few DHS wasting-conflict difference-in-differences studies. The effect is very large.

**Kinyoki, D. K., Moloney, G. M., Uthman, O. A., Kandala, N.-B., Odundo, E. O., Noor, A. M., & Berkley, J. A. (2017). Conflict in Somalia: impact on child undernutrition. *BMJ Global Health*, 2(2), e000262.** https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjgh-2016-000262 [V; volume/article K]
Uses FSNAU nutrition surveys for 73,778 children (2007–2010). Recent conflict: wasting OR 1.37, stunting OR 1.21. Longer-term conflict: wasting OR 1.76, stunting OR 1.88. Effects hold net of drought. *Relevance:* The data come from FSNAU, FEWS NET's partner system in Somalia.

**Ecker, O., Al-Malk, A., & Maystadt, J.-F. (2024). Civil conflict, cash transfers, and child nutrition in Yemen. *Economic Development and Cultural Change*, 72(4), 2069–2100.** https://doi.org/10.1086/726294 [V]
A 1 SD increase in conflict intensity lowers WHZ about 9.6%. The cash transfer program offsets about 42% of that effect.

**Grace, K., Verdin, A., Brown, M., Bakhtsiyarava, M., Backer, D., & Billing, T. (2022). Conflict and climate factors and the risk of child acute malnutrition among children aged 24–59 months: A comparative analysis of Kenya, Nigeria, and Uganda. *Spatial Demography*, 10(2), 329–358.** (PMC10438900) [V]
Uses DHS WHZ with ACLED and climate data. Conflict in the growing season or 1–6 months prior lowers WHZ in Kenya and Nigeria but not Uganda, so the relationships are inconsistent across settings. *Relevance:* This is a wasting-specific DHS study co-authored by Backer and Billing (the FEWS NET validation authors). It is the closest existing work to our proposal.

**Anderson, W., Taylor, C., McDermid, S., Ilboudo-Nébié, E., Seager, R., Schlenker, W., Cottier, F., de Sherbinin, A., Mendeloff, D., & Markey, K. (2021). Violent conflict exacerbated drought-related food insecurity between 2009 and 2019 in sub-Saharan Africa. *Nature Food*, 2, 603–615.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s43016-021-00327-4 [V; author list partly K]
Uses **FEWS NET IPC classifications** as the outcome. The post-2014 rise in food insecurity is attributable to conflict (South Sudan, Nigeria). Pastoral zones stay insecure about 2 years after drought versus about 12 months in agricultural zones. *Relevance:* It shows FEWS NET phases respond to drought and conflict. We would test whether those phases in turn predict anthropometry.

## 4. Famine mortality measurement

**Checchi, F., & Robinson, W. C. (2013). Mortality among populations of southern and central Somalia affected by severe food insecurity and famine during 2010–2012. FAO/FSNAU and FEWS NET, Rome/Washington.** https://fsnau.org/downloads/Somalia_Mortality_Estimates_Final_Report_8May2013_upload.pdf [V]
Estimates about **258,000 excess deaths** (October 2010 to April 2012), including 133,000 children under 5. That is 4.6% of the population and 10% of under-5s in South-Central Somalia. *Relevance:* It shows the forecasts were early and correct but the response was late, and it was commissioned by FEWS NET.

**Maxwell, D., & Fitzpatrick, M. (2012). The 2011 Somalia famine: Context, causes, and complications. *Global Food Security*, 1(1), 5–12.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gfs.2012.07.002 [V]
The famine was caused by drought, conflict, livelihood crisis, and prices. Al-Shabaab access restrictions and counter-terrorism law constrained the response despite early warning.

**Hillbruner, C., & Moloney, G. (2012). When early warning is not enough—Lessons learned from the 2011 Somalia famine. *Global Food Security*, 1(1), 20–28.** [K; title confirmed via search hit]
FEWS NET and FSNAU warned roughly 11 months ahead, and the response still failed.

**de Waal, A. (2018). *Mass Starvation: The History and Future of Famine*. Cambridge: Polity.** [K]
Famine mortality fell sharply after the 1980s, and recent famines are mostly political or conflict-driven ("famine crimes").

**Ó Gráda, C. (2009). *Famine: A Short History*. Princeton: Princeton University Press.** [K]
A standard economic-history reference on famine demography, mortality measurement, and the role of disease vs starvation.

**Howe, P., & Devereux, S. (2004). Famine intensity and magnitude scales: A proposal for an instrumental definition of famine. *Disasters*, 28(4), 353–372.** [K]
The conceptual precursor of IPC phase thresholds, which combine GAM, crude death rate, and food indicators.

## 5. Does humanitarian food aid or cash reduce wasting?

**Langendorf, C., Roederer, T., de Pee, S., Brown, D., Doyon, S., Mamaty, A.-A., et al. (2014). Preventing acute malnutrition among young children in crises: a prospective intervention study in Niger. *PLoS Medicine*, 11(9), e1001714.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001714 [V]
Covers Maradi, children aged 6–23 months, in a lean season. MAM incidence was 7.97 per 100 child-months with cash only versus 3.33 with supplementary food plus cash (HR for cash-only vs SC+/cash = 2.42). SAM was about 3 times higher without cash (HR 3.13). The cash-plus-food combination worked best. This builds on the Niger 2005/2010 MSF lean-season experience.

**Fenn, B., Colbourn, T., Dolan, C., Pietzsch, S., Sangrasi, M., & Shoham, J. (2017). Impact evaluation of different cash-based intervention modalities on child and maternal nutritional status in Sindh Province, Pakistan, at 6 mo and at 1 y: A cluster randomised controlled trial. *PLoS Medicine*, 14(5), e1002305.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002305 [V]
REFANI Pakistan. Only **double-value cash cut wasting odds (OR 0.52)** at 6 months. Standard cash (OR 1.09) and fresh-food vouchers (OR 1.16) had no effect.

**Grijalva-Eternod, C. S., Jelle, M., Haghparast-Bidgoli, H., et al. (2018). A cash-based intervention and the risk of acute malnutrition in children aged 6–59 months living in internally displaced persons camps in Mogadishu, Somalia: A non-randomised cluster trial. *PLoS Medicine*, 15(10), e1002684.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002684 [V]
REFANI Somalia, with $84/month for 5 months. Acute malnutrition was **not reduced** (adjusted HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.51–1.74), although expenditure and food consumption rose.

**Doocy, S., Busingye, M., Lyles, E., Colantouni, E., Aidam, B., Ebulu, G., & Savage, K. (2020). Cash and voucher assistance and children's nutrition status in Somalia. *Maternal & Child Nutrition*, 16(3), e12966.** https://doi.org/10.1111/mcn.12966 [V; author list K]
2017 drought response, with about $450 in transfers. Acute malnutrition was stable among recipients (+0.7 pp with vouchers, −4.8 pp with mixed transfers) while non-recipients deteriorated.

**Kurdi, S., Breisinger, C., Ibrahim, H., Ghorpade, Y., & Al-Ahmadi, A. A. (2021). The nutritional benefits of cash transfers in humanitarian crises: Evidence from Yemen. *World Development*, 148, 105664.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2021.105664 [V-cit; co-authors partly K]
Cash for Nutrition (cash plus nutrition training, soft conditionality). Food purchases rose by at least 17% and animal-source food consumption rose 21 pp, and diagnosed moderate or severe acute malnutrition fell, mostly in the poorest tercile.

**Manley, J., Alderman, H., & Gentilini, U. (2022). More evidence on cash transfers and child nutritional outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *BMJ Global Health*, 7(4), e008233.** https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjgh-2021-008233 [V]
Cash transfers produce small HAZ gains and stunting reductions but **no detectable effect on WAZ or wasting** on average.

**Hidrobo, M., Hoddinott, J., Kumar, N., & Olivier, M. (2018). Social protection, food security, and asset formation. *World Development*, 101, 88–103.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2017.08.014 [V]
Meta-analysis: transfers raise food consumption value by about 13% and calories by about 8%. The effects run through food security, not anthropometry directly.

**Save the Children/Elrha CashPlus trial, Somalia (2025–26). The effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of cash plus interventions in preventing child wasting in Somalia. *Journal of Global Health*, 16, 04111 (2026).** https://jogh.org/2026/jogh-16-04111 [V-cit; authors not recorded]
A three-arm cluster RCT. Cash plus social and behaviour change communication (SBCC) reduced wasting relative to cash alone or cash plus a top-up.

**The "aid dampens measured outcomes" problem.** FEWS NET projections are conditional on anticipated humanitarian assistance: IPC phases can carry a "!" flag meaning the phase would be at least one phase worse without current or programmed assistance. Crises that were forecast and then responded to, such as Ethiopia 2015/16 (Hirvonen et al. 2020), may therefore show small anthropometric effects precisely because the warning worked. Backer & Billing (2021) attribute part of FEWS NET's over-projection at high phases to variation in humanitarian response. The implication is that a naive regression of wasting on projected phase is a reduced-form "forecast plus response" effect, which is policy-relevant but not a structural shock effect. Recording the assistance flag, or WFP/OCHA FTS aid flows, allows heterogeneity tests.

## 6. Measurement issues with DHS anthropometry and seasonality

**Grellety, E., & Golden, M. H. (2016). The effect of random error on diagnostic accuracy illustrated with the anthropometric diagnosis of malnutrition. *PLoS ONE*, 11(12), e0168585.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0168585 [V]
Monte Carlo simulation shows that modest random measurement error can **more than double** measured wasting prevalence. WHZ is especially fragile because height error enters the denominator. *Relevance:* Survey-level noise in wasting is large. Use WHZ means, SD-adjusted prevalence, and survey-quality controls.

**Perumal, N., Namaste, S., Qamar, H., Aimone, A., Bassani, D. G., & Roth, D. E. (2020). Anthropometric data quality assessment in multisurvey studies of child growth. *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, 112(Suppl 2), 806S–815S.** (PMC7487428) [V]
Builds a composite DHS data-quality index (date-of-birth completeness, heaping, implausible values, z-score SD) for HAZ and WHZ separately. *Relevance:* Use it as a control or for sample restriction.

**Larsen, A. F., Headey, D., & Masters, W. A. (2019). Misreporting month of birth: Diagnosis and implications for research on nutrition and early childhood in developing countries. *Demography*, 56(2), 707–728.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s13524-018-0753-9 [V]
Uses 163 DHS surveys in 62 countries. There is a spurious within-year HAZ gradient and a **0.32 HAZ drop from December to January** caused by birth-date misreporting. *Relevance:* This is critical for our stunting cohort-exposure design, since month-of-birth exposure windows are contaminated by age error.

**Venkat, A., Marshak, A., Young, H., & Naumova, E. N. (2023). Seasonality of acute malnutrition in African drylands: Evidence from 15 years of SMART surveys. *Food and Nutrition Bulletin*, 44(2_suppl), S7–S19 (pages K).** https://doi.org/10.1177/03795721231178344 [V-cit]
Uses 412,370 children from SMART surveys. Wasting shows two peaks, April–May (hot season) and August–October (rains). Cross-survey comparisons are biased unless season is addressed. [Note: we found no "Leidman et al." seasonality paper. The user may have in mind Leidman et al. on SMART data quality (Emerging Themes in Epidemiology, 2019) [K], or the Marshak/Venkat papers.]

**Marshak, A., Venkat, A., Young, H., & Naumova, E. N. (2021). How seasonality of malnutrition is measured and analyzed. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 18(4), 1828.** https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18041828 [V]
A review of methods for measuring malnutrition seasonality.

**Shakya, K. S., Bevis, L., Heidkamp, R. A., & Thorne-Lyman, A. L. (2024/2026). How does survey timing influence apparent wasting trends? A case study from Senegal. *Current Developments in Nutrition*, 10(3), 104480.** (PMC13080451) [V; year to confirm]
Senegal continuous DHS 2012–2019. Wasting is 8.8% in the peak season vs 6.4% in the low season, a swing comparable to multi-year change. Month fixed effects fix bias only when survey timing differs by 1–2 months, not when surveys fall in different seasons. *Relevance:* This is directly relevant. DHS fieldwork months are not random, and FEWS NET phases are themselves seasonal, so seasonality confounds both sides.

**Mertens, A., Benjamin-Chung, J., Colford, J. M., et al. (2023). Child wasting and concurrent stunting in low- and middle-income countries. *Nature*, 621, 558–567.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06480-z [V]
Pooled ki cohorts. Wasting incidence is highest in the first months of life and is seasonal. Early wasting predicts later stunting. *Relevance:* It supports treating wasting and stunting as linked, not separate, outcomes.

**Burgert, C. R., Colston, J., Roy, T., & Zachary, B. (2013). Geographic displacement procedure and georeferenced data release policy for the Demographic and Health Surveys. DHS Spatial Analysis Reports No. 7. Calverton, MD: ICF International.** https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/SAR7/SAR7.pdf [V]
DHS cluster GPS points are displaced 0–2 km in urban areas and 0–5 km in rural areas, with 1% of rural clusters displaced up to 10 km, and displacement is constrained to stay within admin-2. *Relevance:* This is minor for FEWS NET livelihood-zone or admin-2 polygons, but matters near boundaries. See also Perez-Heydrich et al. (DHS SAR8, Guidelines on the use of DHS GPS data) [V-cit].

**Survey gaps in conflict** [K; no single paper verified]. DHS surveys are sparse or absent precisely where FEWS NET phases are highest: Somalia (no standard DHS until 2018–19 SHDS), South Sudan, Yemen (2013 only), northeast Nigeria (security-restricted sampling), and Sudan. Selection on survey feasibility attenuates estimated shock effects. SMART surveys and FSNAU data are the complements. Kinyoki et al. (2017) and Dunn (2018) illustrate this.

## 7. Machine-learning and geospatial prediction of wasting

**Local Burden of Disease Child Growth Failure Collaborators (Kinyoki, D. K., et al.) (2020). Mapping child growth failure across low- and middle-income countries. *Nature*, 577, 231–234.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1878-8 [V]
5×5 km estimates for 105 LMICs, 2000–2017. Stunting fell from 36.9% to 26.6%, and wasting fell from **8.4% to 6.4%**. More than half of LMICs are projected to miss the <5% wasting target in 2025.

**Kinyoki, D. K., Osgood-Zimmerman, A. E., Pickering, B. V., et al. (2020). Mapping local patterns of childhood overweight and wasting in low- and middle-income countries between 2000 and 2017. *Nature Medicine*, 26, 750–759.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-020-0807-6 [V-cit]
Wasting-specific local burden-of-disease maps.

**Osgood-Zimmerman, A., et al. (2018). Mapping child growth failure in Africa between 2000 and 2015. *Nature*, 555, 41–47.** https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25760 [V-cit]
The African precursor of the 2020 maps.

*Relevance of the LBD maps:* They use smooth spatiotemporal models with covariates such as rainfall, NDVI, and conflict. They are poor at capturing short-run shocks because annual smoothing flattens crisis spikes. A FEWS NET-based wasting model could be framed as a "shock layer" on top of the LBD baseline.

**Lentz, E. C., Michelson, H., Baylis, K., & Zhou, Y. (2019). A data-driven approach improves food insecurity crisis prediction. *World Development*, 122, 399–409.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2019.06.008 [V]
In Malawi, models built on public data predict 65–88% of village-cluster food security status out of sample and 83–99% of the most insecure clusters. This is a benchmark for "cheap data vs expert forecast".

## 8. FEWS NET accuracy (context for the forecast-vs-raw-shock question)

**Backer, D., & Billing, T. (2021). Validating Famine Early Warning Systems Network projections of food security in Africa, 2009–2020. *Global Food Security*, 29, 100510.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gfs.2021.100510 [V]
Covers 25 countries. Accuracy is 84% overall but falls sharply at higher phases, with a bias toward over-projection. The pattern is linked to humanitarian response, climate, and conflict.

**Choularton, R. J., & Krishnamurthy, P. K. (2019). How accurate is food security early warning? Evaluation of FEWS NET accuracy in Ethiopia. *Food Security*, 11, 333–344.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s12571-019-00909-y [V]
Ethiopia, 2011–2017. Accuracy is generally high but lower in extreme events (2015/16 El Niño), in data-scarce areas, and at transitions into crisis.

**Coughlan de Perez, E., van Aalst, M., Choularton, R., van den Hurk, B., Mason, S., Nissan, H., & Schwager, S. (2019). From rain to famine: Assessing the utility of rainfall observations and seasonal forecasts to anticipate food insecurity in East Africa. *Food Security*, 11(1), 57–68 (pages K).** https://doi.org/10.1007/s12571-018-00885-9 [V]
Uses FEWS NET IPC as the outcome. Six to twelve months of rainfall observations give about six months' warning, especially in pastoral zones. Seasonal forecasts add value only when they strongly signal below-normal rain. *Relevance:* This is the direct "raw rainfall vs forecast" comparison, but for IPC outcomes, not anthropometry.

## Synthesis (about 480 words)

**What is established.** Our paper should not present "droughts raise stunting" or "shocks raise wasting" as findings. Multi-country DHS designs already show that rainfall deficits (Cooper et al. 2019, 53 countries), ENSO (Anttila-Hughes et al. 2021), heat (Blom et al. 2022; Baker & Anttila-Hughes 2020), national income falls (Headey & Ruel 2022), and food inflation (Headey & Ruel 2023) worsen child anthropometry. Early-life exposure has lasting height costs (Hoddinott & Kinsey 2001; Dercon & Porter 2014; Maccini & Yang 2009). Conflict lowers HAZ in Rwanda, Burundi, and Côte d'Ivoire, and lowers WHZ in Nigeria, Somalia, and Yemen.

**Typical magnitudes.** At the population level, climate and price effects on stunting are modest. Early-life drought typically moves HAZ by a few hundredths to about 0.15 SD, and 1 SD of rainfall moves HAZ 4–13% in Shively (2017). Wasting elasticities are larger in relative terms: a 10% GDP fall raises wasting 14–18%, a 5% real food price rise over 3 months raises it 9% (14% for severe wasting), and extreme heat lowers WHZ about 0.16 SD. Conflict and famine effects are an order of magnitude larger: HAZ 0.25–1.05 SD lower, WHZ 0.49 SD lower, wasting +13 pp in northeast Nigeria, and at least 5 cm of adult height lost from the 1984 famine. Wasting effects are noisier and often null (the ENSO WHZ null; Uganda in Grace et al. 2022), consistent with measurement error (Grellety & Golden 2016) and seasonality (Shakya et al.; Venkat et al.).

**Gaps FEWS NET could fill.**
1. *An integrated shock measure.* Existing papers study one shock channel at a time. FEWS NET phases are an expert synthesis of rainfall, prices, conflict, livestock, and access, which is exactly what the single-shock literature cannot capture (Anderson et al. 2021 show phases respond to both drought and conflict).
2. *Sub-national, sub-annual resolution.* Headey & Ruel's price and income shocks are national. FEWS NET gives admin-2 or livelihood-zone phases 3–4 times a year, matching the short window over which wasting responds.
3. *The forecast horse-race question.* No paper we found tests whether FEWS NET projections, or current classifications, predict anthropometry better than raw SPEI/CHIRPS, prices, or ACLED events. The closest work, Coughlan de Perez et al. (2019) and Backer & Billing (2021), validates forecasts against later IPC classifications, not biological outcomes. A natural design is to regress DHS WHZ or wasting on (a) the projected phase made 4–8 months before interview, (b) the realized current phase, and (c) the raw shock vector, and compare out-of-sample fit. Our prior is that FEWS NET should beat raw rainfall in conflict and pastoral settings, where rainfall alone misleads, and add little in rainfed-crop settings.
4. *Aid endogeneity.* Because projections anticipate assistance and warnings trigger response (Ethiopia 2015: Hirvonen et al. 2020), the phase–wasting gradient is a reduced-form effect of "forecast plus response". Heterogeneity by the "!" assistance flag or by aid flows is informative in itself.
5. *Stunting cohorts.* Following Headey & Ruel (2023), cumulative in-utero and first-1,000-days exposure to Phase 3+ is a natural stunting regressor. It needs corrections for age heaping (Larsen et al. 2019) and mortality selection (Kudamatsu et al.; Baird et al.).

The main threats are sparse DHS coverage in the worst-hit areas, seasonal confounding between fieldwork timing and FEWS NET's lean-season calendar, and noise in WHZ.
